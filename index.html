Income, Industry & Education in Denmark

A comprehensive analysis of socioeconomic factors, industry, and wealth across municipalities.

Introduction

Welcome to our final project for the DTU Social Data course. In this data story, we examine how income, industry, and education interact across Denmark's municipalities, drawing on data from Statistics Denmark (HFUDD16, IFOR35) and the Central Business Register (CVR).

Three questions guide our analysis. Part 1 asks how income and demographics are distributed geographically, finding that wealth differs across the country, but the underlying makeup of municipalities (who works, who studies, who is unemployed) is strikingly uniform. Part 2 asks whether the physical churn of business, the opening and closing of production units, explains that wealth, and finds that it largely does not. Part 3 asks what does, examining how education levels and industry composition shape local labour markets.

Together, the three parts argue that Denmark's economic geography is not a story of where companies physically locate, but of what kind of work happens inside them, and who is educated to do it.

Part 1: The Geographical Distribution of Income & Demographics

National trend

From 2008 to 2024, average incomes across Danish municipalities have shown a steady, consistent upward trajectory. This general increase aligns closely with standard economic inflation over the 16-year period. Rather than localized, explosive economic booms, the data establishes a stable baseline of wealth growth nationwide.

Demographic Uniformity

Despite differences in overall wealth, municipalities share a consistent socioeconomic profile. The proportions of employed, unemployed, and enrolled individuals changes only slightly across the country, as you can see in the ternary plot below where all the data points lie on a thin strip of the chart space. This suggests that average municipal income is largely independent of the size of the workforce, but rather dependent on the types of local industries.

The Vejen & Billund Anomalies

While national trends are steady, average incomes in Vejen and Billund experienced an anomalous spike between 2022 and 2023, jumping from 314k to 446k DKK and from 329k to 470k DKK, respectively. Following this surge, however, their trajectories diverged: Billund's average income corrected downward to 340k DKK the following year, while Vejen's remained elevated, stabilizing at 435k DKK.

Part 2: Production Units and the Demographic Map

Where Units Open, People Follow, A Little

Part 1 found that Danish municipalities look demographically similar on the surface. We expected the physical economy, the opening and closing of production units, to differentiate them more. Across 98 municipalities from 2008 to 2024, the net change in active production units correlates positively with population growth (Pearson r = 0.39, p < 0.001), but explains only about 15% of the variance. The relationship is real and statistically robust, yet most of what moves Danes between municipalities happens for other reasons.

Income Doesn't Follow At All

If unit churn weakly predicts where people live, it says almost nothing about how much they earn. The correlation with income growth is r = -0.077 (p = 0.45), statistically indistinguishable from zero. Vejen and Billund, flagged as income anomalies in Part 1, illustrate the point: Vejen lost production units (32% decline) while average income rose 75%, and Billund's modest unit growth on a small base does not explain its 2022 to 2023 income surge. Wealth in a Danish municipality reflects who lives there, not what is built there.

Geography Beats Churn

The dominant pattern is geographic, not industrial. Five of the ten municipalities with the largest unit losses are islands or peripheral kommuner, namely Læsø, Samsø, Ærø, Fanø, and Tønder, and these are precisely the places losing population (Læsø 12% decline, Ærø 11% decline, Tønder 9% decline). One counter-example sharpens the point: Kalundborg added 52 net units (a 433% increase), driven by the Novo Nordisk and Novozymes industrial cluster, yet lost 2.9% of its population. The new jobs were largely filled by commuters from neighbouring kommuner, not by new residents. Production unit counts shape Denmark at the margins; to explain wealth, we need to look inside the units, at industry and education, which Part 3 examines.

Limitations and Further Work

This analysis treats production units as interchangeable counts. In reality a 500 employee pharmaceutical plant and a three person consultancy have very different local impact. The CVR employee field is too sparsely populated in our data to weight units by size, and very small kommuner produce volatile percentage changes (Vallensbæk grew from 1 to 7 units, a 600% increase that reflects sample size more than economic transformation). A natural extension is to classify units by industry sector (NACE branchekoder) and ask whether what opens matters more than how many. Given Part 3's finding that knowledge intensive sectors track education, this is likely where the strongest explanatory signal lies.

Part 3: How Education Shapes Denmark’s Labour Market

A More Educated Denmark

Across Denmark, the data shows a clear shift toward higher educational attainment over time. A larger share of the population get a master’s degree and PhD's. At the same time, industries such as Consultancy, R&D and Business Services grow, while Education, Health, Arts and Recreation remain major sectors. This suggests that a more highly educated population is increasingly linked to knowledge-intensive and public-service industries.

Lyngby-Taarbæk and Kolding Follow Different Paths

Comparing Lyngby-Taarbæk and Kolding highlights two different municipal profiles. Lyngby-Taarbæk has a higher share of people with master’s and PhD degrees, reflecting its strong connection to education, research and consultancy. Kolding has a larger share of people with bachelor-level or lower education across many industries, pointing to a more industrial and business-oriented structure. Still, both municipalities show a rise in master’s degree holders over time.

Education Helps Explain Industry and Employment

Kolding has a higher employed share across much of the period, but employment also fluctuates more, especially after the financial crisis in 2008, likely because manufacturing and production-related industries play a larger role. Lyngby-Taarbæk shows a more stable pattern, probably because larger proportion of population are linked to education, research and knowledge-intensive industries. Together, the comparison shows how education levels help shape local industry structure and labour-market patterns across Denmark.

Conclusion

The three parts of this project point in the same direction. Average income in Denmark has risen steadily across all municipalities, yet the gap between wealthier and poorer kommuner has not closed. Part 1 established that this gap cannot be attributed to the size of the local workforce, since the proportions of employed, unemployed, and enrolled residents remain remarkably similar across the country. The source of the wealth difference must therefore lie in factors beyond the share of the population in work.

Part 2 examined one plausible candidate, namely the physical presence of business as measured by the opening and closing of production units. The results revealed a moderate but limited association with population change (r = 0.39) and no meaningful association with income (r = -0.077). A clear geographic pattern did emerge, separating the urban centres, which tend to gain both units and residents, from the island and peripheral kommuner, which tend to lose both. The number of production units in a municipality, however, provides little explanation for differences in earnings.

Part 3 offered a more compelling account. Education levels and industry composition vary substantially between municipalities, and the two appear to move in parallel. Lyngby-Taarbæk's concentration of master's and PhD holders corresponds to its strength in consultancy, research, and other knowledge-intensive sectors, while Kolding's lower average attainment corresponds to a stronger manufacturing and production base. The type of work available in a municipality, together with the educational profile of its residents, tracks local economic outcomes more closely than any of the physical or demographic measures considered in earlier parts.

We remain cautious about the strength of these conclusions. The analysis is correlational throughout, the observation window is short relative to the timescales on which demographic change unfolds, and our treatment of production units does not account for employment size or industry sector. What we can state with reasonable confidence is that the simple narrative, in which wealthy municipalities are wealthy because more businesses have located there, is not supported by our data. A more productive line of enquiry concerns the kind of work performed in a place and the education of those who perform it. This is where future analysis of Danish economic geography is likely to yield the strongest insights.
